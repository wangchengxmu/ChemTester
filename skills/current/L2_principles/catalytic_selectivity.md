# Catalytic Selectivity & Shape-Selective Zeolites

## Core Concept

Catalytic selectivity — the ability to favor one product over others — is critical in industrial chemistry. Zeolites achieve selectivity through their molecular-sized pores that discriminate reactants, products, or transition states by size and shape.

---

## Types of Selectivity

### 1. Chemoselectivity
Different functional groups react at different rates. E.g., hydrogenation of C=C vs C=O.

### 2. Regioselectivity
Reaction occurs preferentially at one position. E.g., para-selective alkylation.

### 3. Stereoselectivity
One stereoisomer is favored. E.g., asymmetric hydrogenation.

### 4. Shape Selectivity (Zeolites)
Molecular sieving based on pore dimensions.

---

## Zeolites: Molecular Sieve Catalysts

### Structure
- Aluminosilicate frameworks: [SiO₄]⁴⁻ and [AlO₄]⁵⁻ tetrahedra
- Regular pore system with molecular dimensions (3–13 Å)
- Framework types: FAU (X, Y), MFI (ZSM-5), MOR, BEA, LTA

### Key Zeolites

| Zeolite | Pore Size | Si/Al | Major Use |
|---------|-----------|-------|-----------|
| ZSM-5 (MFI) | 5.5 Å (10-ring) | 10–∞ | FCC, MTG, xylene isomerization |
| Zeolite Y (FAU) | 7.4 Å (12-ring) | 1.5–∞ | FCC cracking |
| Zeolite β (BEA) | 6.6 × 6.7 Å | 5–∞ | Alkylation, acylation |
| Mordenite (MOR) | 2.6 × 5.7 Å | 5–∞ | Hydroisomerization |
| SAPO-34 | 3.8 Å (8-ring) | — | MTO (methanol-to-olefins) |

---

## Types of Shape Selectivity

### Reactant Selectivity
Only molecules small enough to enter pores can react.
- Example: n-hexane isomerized in ZSM-5 but bulky iso-hexanes cannot enter.

### Product Selectivity
Only products small enough to diffuse out are formed.
- Example: ZSM-5 limits benzene alkylation to p-xylene (para) because o- and m-xylene cannot escape easily.

### Transition State Selectivity
Certain transition states are too large to form within the pore cavity.
- Example: disproportionation suppressed in medium-pore zeolites.

---

## Brønsted Acidity in Zeolites

Substitution of Si⁴⁺ by Al³⁺ creates a negative charge, balanced by a proton:

$$\text{≡Si–OH–Al≡}$$

This bridging hydroxyl is a strong Brønsted acid site (similar strength to H₂SO₄).

### Acid site density
$$\text{[acid sites]} = \frac{\text{[Al]}}{\text{unit cell}}$$

Higher Si/Al ratio → fewer acid sites → less coking.
